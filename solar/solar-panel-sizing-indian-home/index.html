SOLAR / ROOFTOP PV DESIGN

How to Size a Rooftop Solar System for an Indian Home

A practical first-pass method to estimate rooftop solar capacity from your electricity consumption. Learn how daily energy, local solar resource, system losses, module wattage, roof conditions and inverter limits fit together.

Quick sizing formula

Estimated PV capacity (kW) = Daily energy requirement (kWh) ÷ [Peak sun hours × Performance ratio]

This is a planning estimate, not a final system design. Use a location-specific solar-yield assessment and confirm equipment and utility requirements before installation.

1. Start with your electricity bills

Collect at least 12 months of electricity bills if available. Record monthly kWh consumption and identify seasonal changes. Convert a representative monthly figure into average daily energy:

Average daily energy (kWh/day) = Monthly consumption (kWh) ÷ Number of days in that month

Example: 300 kWh in a 30-day month = 10 kWh/day.

Do not size a system from the sanctioned load or the sum of appliance nameplate ratings alone. Actual energy use, daytime consumption, future load changes and the utility's permitted system capacity all matter.

2. Estimate the solar resource and system losses

Peak sun hours (PSH) represent the equivalent number of hours per day at standard test irradiance. They are not the same as daylight hours. PSH varies by location, season, weather and the plane in which the modules are installed. Use a credible location-specific solar resource tool or a qualified designer's yield report for the site.

The performance ratio (PR) is a simplifying way to account for real-world losses such as module temperature, inverter conversion, wiring, soiling, mismatch and availability. For an illustrative calculation, this guide uses 4.5 PSH and a PR of 0.75. These are assumptions for the example only, not guaranteed values for Chennai or any other location.

3. Worked example: a home using 300 kWh per month

Assume a 30-day month and the illustrative solar conditions stated above:

  • Monthly electricity use = 300 kWh
  • Average daily energy = 300 ÷ 30 = 10 kWh/day
  • Illustrative peak sun hours = 4.5 hours/day
  • Illustrative performance ratio = 0.75

Estimated PV capacity = 10 ÷ (4.5 × 0.75) = approximately 2.96 kW.

A preliminary estimate is therefore around 3 kWp. Actual system capacity should be checked against the site's expected generation, roof layout, inverter specifications, connection rules and the household's consumption pattern. Solar production will not exactly match consumption in every month or hour.

4. Estimate the number of solar panels

Divide the proposed DC array capacity by the rated wattage of the chosen module:

Panel count = Required array capacity (W) ÷ Module rating (W)

For the example, a 3,000 W array using 550 W modules gives 3,000 ÷ 550 = 5.45 modules. Since modules are installed as whole units, six 550 W modules make a 3.3 kWp DC array. The designer must then verify that this array is compatible with the inverter's permitted DC capacity, voltage and current limits.

5. Check usable rooftop area and shading

Use the module manufacturer's dimensions to calculate the panel footprint. Then allow additional space for row spacing, access paths, maintenance, parapets, setbacks and safe working clearances. The usable area is not simply the total roof area.

  • Identify shadows from water tanks, staircases, trees, nearby buildings and parapet walls throughout the day and across seasons.
  • Check roof orientation, tilt, structural condition, waterproofing and wind-loading requirements.
  • Plan safe access for cleaning and maintenance without blocking drainage or access routes.
  • Ask the installer for a roof layout showing module dimensions, spacing and maintenance pathways.

Even partial shading can reduce output, depending on module wiring, bypass diodes, string design and the inverter or optimiser architecture. A site survey is important before finalising the layout.

6. Choose the inverter and system type

A grid-connected system, a hybrid system and an off-grid system are not interchangeable:

  • Grid-tied: Usually designed to supply household loads while connected to the grid and export eligible surplus under the applicable arrangement. A conventional grid-tied inverter normally shuts down during a grid outage for anti-islanding safety; it does not automatically provide backup.
  • Hybrid: Can coordinate solar, grid and battery storage when designed with compatible equipment. Confirm which circuits receive backup and the inverter's backup power limits.
  • Off-grid: Designed to operate without a utility grid and requires careful sizing of PV, battery storage and backup generation, if any.

Do not choose an inverter only by matching its kW label to the panel total. Check the manufacturer's permitted PV oversizing, maximum DC voltage, MPPT voltage range, input current, short-circuit current, number of strings, AC output and grid-code requirements. The final DC-to-AC ratio and string arrangement must follow the exact inverter datasheet.

7. Do you need a battery?

A battery is not automatically required for a grid-connected bill-saving system. Decide whether your priority is reducing grid energy purchases, backup during outages or independent off-grid operation. For backup, list the essential loads, their measured or realistic power demand and the hours of autonomy required.

Battery sizing must account for usable depth of discharge, round-trip efficiency, inverter limits, battery chemistry, temperature, ageing, discharge rate and the manufacturer's operating limits. A larger battery adds cost and does not guarantee that every household circuit can run during an outage.

8. Understand generation, self-consumption and bill savings

Annual or monthly solar generation is not the same as bill savings. Savings depend on when the home uses electricity, how much solar energy is consumed directly, how exported energy is credited, applicable tariff components and the utility's current metering arrangement.

Before investing, ask for a month-by-month generation estimate and a clear explanation of assumptions. Compare the estimate with your actual bills and check the current rules with your distribution utility. Do not assume that every exported unit receives the same value as an imported unit.

9. India-specific checks before installation

  • Confirm the current rooftop-solar application process, eligible system capacity, metering arrangement and technical requirements with your local distribution company.
  • For Tamil Nadu, verify the latest process and applicable charges or conditions directly with the relevant TANGEDCO/TNPDCL office or official portal, as procedures and regulations can change.
  • If considering a residential subsidy, check current eligibility, approved vendor requirements, capacity limits and application steps on the official national rooftop-solar portal. Do not rely on old subsidy figures or an installer's verbal promise.
  • Obtain the equipment datasheets, warranties, protection scheme, earthing and lightning-protection design where required, commissioning documents and monitoring access.
  • Use qualified professionals for structural assessment, electrical design, installation, testing and grid synchronisation.

10. Solar sizing checklist

  • Review 12 months of electricity consumption and identify daytime loads.
  • Estimate daily kWh from actual bills.
  • Obtain a location- and roof-specific solar-yield estimate.
  • Calculate a preliminary PV capacity using explicit loss assumptions.
  • Confirm panel count, module dimensions, roof area and shading.
  • Verify inverter DC and AC limits, string design and protection.
  • Decide whether battery backup is genuinely needed and size it from essential loads.
  • Confirm utility approval, metering, current subsidy eligibility and total project cost.

Frequently asked questions

How many solar panels are needed for a 300-unit monthly bill?

There is no single panel count based on units alone. Convert the bill to average daily kWh, use a site-specific solar-yield estimate and performance assumptions to calculate array capacity, then divide by the selected module wattage. The worked example above illustrates the method, not a guaranteed design.

Will a 3 kW solar system generate 3 kWh every hour?

No. 3 kW is the system's rated power under standard test conditions. Actual output changes with irradiance, module temperature, shading, orientation, inverter behaviour and system losses.

Can rooftop solar run during a power cut?

A standard grid-tied inverter generally disconnects during a grid outage for safety. Backup requires a compatible hybrid or other approved arrangement with correctly configured backup circuits and, where needed, batteries.

Is roof area the only factor in solar system size?

No. Consumption, solar resource, shading, roof structure, inverter limits, utility rules and the economics of self-consumption and export all affect the final system size.
