BACKUP POWER / UPS & BATTERIES

How to Calculate UPS Battery Backup Time

A practical guide to estimating how long a UPS battery bank can support a load. Understand battery voltage, ampere-hours, watt-hours, inverter efficiency and why actual runtime is usually different from a simple calculation.

Quick estimate

Approximate AC energy (Wh) = Battery voltage (V) × Capacity (Ah) × Usable discharge fraction × Inverter efficiency

Idealised runtime (hours) = Approximate AC energy (Wh) ÷ AC load (W)

This is an energy estimate, not a guaranteed runtime or battery-design method. Battery discharge characteristics and UPS cut-off behaviour can materially change the result.

1. Identify the actual load

Start by listing the equipment that must remain powered during an outage. Measure or obtain the real power in watts (W), not just the equipment's VA rating. Add the simultaneous running loads and consider starting surges, power factor, crest factor and any planned load growth.

For a UPS, verify both its rated output in watts and volt-amperes (VA). A UPS can reach its watt limit before its VA limit, or vice versa. Motors, compressors and other equipment with high starting current may need special consideration and may not be suitable for a small UPS.

2. Understand battery voltage and Ah capacity

Battery bank voltage is the nominal DC voltage of the complete bank connected to the UPS. Battery capacity in ampere-hours (Ah) is normally specified at a stated discharge rate, temperature and end voltage. It is not a fixed amount of energy available at every load.

For a simple nominal-energy estimate:

Nominal battery energy (Wh) = DC battery bank voltage × Ah capacity

Example: 192 V × 100 Ah = 19,200 Wh, or 19.2 kWh nominal.

Do not assume the full nominal energy can be delivered to the AC load. The UPS has conversion losses and the battery must remain within its permitted discharge and cut-off limits.

3. Apply usable discharge and inverter efficiency

For an initial estimate, apply a usable discharge fraction and an assumed UPS efficiency:

Estimated usable AC energy = V × Ah × usable fraction × efficiency

Illustrative example: 192 V battery bank, 100 Ah, 80% usable discharge fraction and 90% inverter efficiency:

19,200 Wh × 0.80 × 0.90 = 13,824 Wh of estimated AC energy.

The 80% and 90% figures are example assumptions only. Use the battery and UPS manufacturer's specified limits and performance data for an actual installation.

4. Calculate idealised runtime

Divide the estimated usable AC energy by the load in watts:

Runtime (hours) = Usable AC energy (Wh) ÷ Load (W)

If the example bank supports a constant 3,000 W load:

13,824 Wh ÷ 3,000 W = 4.61 hours as an idealised energy estimate.

Do not treat 4.61 hours as a guaranteed UPS runtime. The actual result can be significantly shorter, particularly at high discharge rates or with an aged battery bank.

5. Why actual UPS runtime is different

  • Discharge rate: Lead-acid battery capacity is commonly rated at a specified hour rate. Higher current draw reduces available capacity due to the Peukert effect.
  • Battery chemistry: Lead-acid, lithium-ion and other battery types have different voltage curves, usable energy limits and management requirements.
  • Battery age and condition: Capacity declines with ageing, cycling, poor charging, high temperature and maintenance issues.
  • Temperature: Battery performance and service life are temperature dependent. Follow the manufacturer's recommended operating range.
  • UPS efficiency: Conversion efficiency varies with load level and operating mode; a single percentage is only an approximation.
  • Cut-off voltage: The UPS disconnects when its DC input reaches a configured limit to protect the battery and equipment.
  • Wiring and connections: Voltage drop, cable losses and connection condition can affect delivered power and shutdown behaviour.
  • Load variation: Real equipment may cycle, change operating mode or draw brief surge currents.

6. How to size a battery bank from a required backup time

For a first-pass estimate, rearrange the energy relationship:

Required nominal battery energy (Wh) ≈ Load (W) × Backup time (h) ÷ (usable fraction × efficiency)

Then divide by the nominal DC bank voltage to obtain an approximate Ah requirement:

Required Ah ≈ Required nominal battery energy (Wh) ÷ Battery bank voltage (V)

Example: a 1,000 W load for 2 hours, with an illustrative 80% usable fraction and 90% efficiency, at 48 V DC:

  • Required nominal energy ≈ 1,000 × 2 ÷ (0.80 × 0.90) = 2,778 Wh
  • Estimated capacity ≈ 2,778 ÷ 48 = 57.9 Ah

This is only an initial energy calculation. The final battery capacity must be selected from manufacturer discharge tables at the required discharge rate and end voltage, with allowance for ageing, temperature, design margin, DC current, cable sizing, protection and UPS compatibility. Never select a battery bank from this simplified formula alone.

7. Check the UPS and battery configuration

  • Confirm the UPS DC bus voltage and the number of batteries or battery modules required in series.
  • Confirm the permitted battery chemistry, capacity range, charging profile and battery management interface where applicable.
  • Check the UPS maximum battery charging current and expected recharge time after an outage.
  • Verify the manufacturer's battery cabinet, fuse or DC breaker, isolator, cable and ventilation requirements.
  • For parallel battery strings, confirm that the UPS manufacturer permits the arrangement and specifies protection and interconnection requirements.
  • Ensure the UPS is not overloaded in watts or VA and that the load's starting characteristics are supported.

8. Battery safety and maintenance

UPS battery systems can deliver very high fault current and may contain hazardous electrolyte or stored electrical energy. Use suitable enclosures, protection, ventilation and access controls as specified by the manufacturer. Do not short battery terminals, mix old and new batteries in a string, or change series/parallel arrangements without an approved design.

Maintenance should include inspection of battery condition, alarms, temperature, charger operation, connections and event logs according to the manufacturer's schedule. Capacity or runtime testing should use an approved procedure and appropriate instruments, performed by qualified personnel.

Frequently asked questions

Does a 100 Ah battery always provide 100 A for one hour?

No. The rated Ah capacity depends on the specified discharge rate, temperature and end voltage. At a high discharge current, the available capacity may be lower than the nameplate value.

Can I calculate UPS runtime using only battery Ah?

No. You also need the battery bank voltage, actual load, usable discharge limits and conversion losses. Battery discharge characteristics and UPS cut-off settings are also important.

Why does my UPS give less backup than the calculation?

Possible reasons include battery ageing, high load, high discharge rate, incorrect battery configuration, low temperature or high temperature, poor connections, charger issues and UPS cut-off settings. Check the manufacturer's diagnostics and arrange a qualified inspection.

Can I add more batteries to increase backup time?

Only if the UPS is designed to support the proposed battery capacity and configuration. Confirm DC bus voltage, charger capacity, protection, wiring, battery compatibility and manufacturer approval first.
