MCB vs MCCB vs RCCB: What Is the Difference?

MCB, MCCB and RCCB devices may look similar in a distribution panel, but they perform different protection functions. This guide explains what each device does, where it is commonly used and what engineers should consider during selection.

Quick comparison

MCB: Protects a circuit against overload and short-circuit overcurrent.

MCCB: Provides overcurrent protection for higher-current applications, often with a wider range of ratings and optional adjustable trip settings.

RCCB: Detects residual current (earth-leakage imbalance). It does not, by itself, provide overload or short-circuit protection.

1. What is an MCB?

An MCB (Miniature Circuit Breaker) is an automatically operated switch that interrupts a circuit when overcurrent conditions occur. Its thermal mechanism responds to sustained overload, while its magnetic mechanism responds rapidly to high short-circuit current.

MCBs are commonly used in final distribution circuits in homes, offices and smaller commercial installations. They are available with different rated currents, pole configurations, trip characteristics and short-circuit breaking capacities.

What to check when selecting an MCB

  • Rated current in relation to the design load and the current-carrying capacity of the cable.
  • Rated voltage and number of poles appropriate to the supply and circuit.
  • Breaking capacity suitable for the prospective short-circuit current at the installation point.
  • Trip characteristic appropriate to the load's starting or inrush current, without compromising cable and fault protection.
  • Coordination with upstream and downstream protective devices.

2. What is an MCCB?

An MCCB (Moulded Case Circuit Breaker) is an overcurrent protective device commonly used in feeders, distribution boards and larger electrical systems. Compared with many MCB ranges, MCCBs typically cover higher current ratings and may offer adjustable overload and short-circuit trip settings, accessories and a wider range of breaking capacities.

Not every MCCB has the same adjustment features. Some models use fixed thermal-magnetic trips, while others use electronic trip units. Always check the exact manufacturer's data sheet and trip-unit specification.

What to check when selecting an MCCB

  • Frame size and actual rated or sensor current, plus the trip-unit rating and settings.
  • Overload and short-circuit protection characteristics and the available adjustment range.
  • Ultimate and service short-circuit breaking capacities, as applicable to the design.
  • Number of poles, rated operational voltage and installation category.
  • Selective coordination, backup protection and compatibility with downstream devices.
  • Accessories such as auxiliary contacts, shunt trip or undervoltage release, if the control scheme requires them.

3. What is an RCCB?

An RCCB (Residual Current Circuit Breaker) monitors the current flowing through the live conductors and detects an imbalance that indicates some current may be flowing through an unintended path, such as earth. It disconnects the circuit when the residual current reaches its specified operating conditions.

RCCBs are used as part of protection against electric shock and earth-leakage-related hazards. The required residual operating current, device type and disconnection arrangement depend on the installation, connected equipment and applicable rules.

Important: RCCB is not an overcurrent breaker

A standard RCCB does not provide overload or short-circuit protection. It must be coordinated with a suitable overcurrent protective device, such as an MCB or fuse, unless the chosen product is a combined device specifically designed to provide both functions.

What to check when selecting an RCCB

  • Rated current (In) and rated residual operating current (IΔn) are different specifications; check both.
  • Device type (for example, AC, A, F or B) must suit the residual-current waveforms that connected loads can produce. Follow equipment and device manufacturer guidance.
  • Number of poles and neutral switching arrangement must match the circuit configuration.
  • Conditional short-circuit rating and required upstream overcurrent protection.
  • Time-delay or selectivity requirements where multiple residual-current devices are installed in series.
  • Compatibility with the installation's earthing system and applicable regulations.

5. What is an RCBO?

An RCBO combines residual-current protection with overcurrent protection in one device. It is designed to provide the functions of an RCCB and an MCB for an individual circuit, subject to the device's ratings and specifications. Check its rated current, residual operating current, breaking capacity, poles and residual-current type before selection.

6. Practical examples

  • Lighting or socket final circuit: An appropriately selected MCB may provide overload and short-circuit protection. Additional residual-current protection may also be required by the installation design and rules.
  • Main or sub-main feeder: An MCCB may be selected where its current rating, trip characteristics and breaking capacity suit the feeder and coordination study.
  • Additional shock protection: An RCCB or RCBO may be used where required, with sensitivity and type selected for the circuit and connected loads.

These are examples, not a design prescription. The correct arrangement depends on the supply system, earthing, cable sizing, prospective fault current, load characteristics, discrimination and local requirements.

7. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing a breaker only by matching its ampere rating to the load.
  • Ignoring the prospective short-circuit current and the device's breaking capacity.
  • Assuming an RCCB protects against overload or short circuit.
  • Assuming every RCCB type is suitable for every electronic load or inverter-based appliance.
  • Using an RCCB as a replacement for protective earthing or bonding.
  • Changing trip settings without checking conductor protection and coordination.
  • Mixing neutrals between circuits protected by different RCCBs, which can cause unwanted tripping or unsafe arrangements.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use an RCCB without an MCB?

A standard RCCB needs suitable overcurrent protection upstream or downstream as part of the designed arrangement. An RCCB alone does not protect the circuit against overload or short circuit.

Is an MCCB always better than an MCB?

No. The device should match the circuit's load, fault level, installation conditions and coordination requirements. A correctly selected MCB is appropriate for many final circuits; an MCCB is often used for larger feeders and distribution applications.

Does an RCCB replace earthing?

No. Residual-current protection is an additional protective measure and does not replace a correctly designed protective earthing and bonding system.

What is the difference between RCCB and RCBO?

An RCCB detects residual current but does not provide overcurrent protection. An RCBO combines residual-current and overcurrent protection in one device.

Final selection checklist

  • Confirm supply voltage, phase arrangement and earthing system.
  • Calculate design current and verify cable current-carrying capacity.
  • Determine prospective short-circuit current at the installation point.
  • Select suitable trip characteristics and breaking capacity.
  • Assess the need for residual-current protection and choose the correct device type and sensitivity.
  • Check coordination, selectivity, manufacturer's instructions and applicable electrical standards.
