How to Calculate Three-Phase Electricity Consumption

A practical guide to estimating three-phase real power in kilowatts (kW) and electrical energy in kilowatt-hours (kWh), using voltage, current, power factor and operating time.

Quick answer: For a reasonably balanced three-phase load, real power is calculated using line-to-line voltage, line current and power factor:

Three-phase real power

kW = (√3 × Line voltage × Line current × Power factor) ÷ 1,000

Energy (kWh) = Real power (kW) × Operating time (hours)

1. Understand the values

  • Line voltage (V): Voltage measured between any two phases. A common nominal low-voltage value in many Indian installations is 415 V, but use the actual measured or specified supply voltage.
  • Line current (A): Current measured in each line conductor. For a balanced load, the three line currents are approximately equal.
  • Power factor (PF): The ratio of real power to apparent power. Use the actual power factor reading where available; do not assume it is always 1.
  • Operating time: The number of hours the load operates during the period being estimated.

2. Worked example

Assume a balanced three-phase load with these illustrative readings:

  • Line voltage = 415 V
  • Line current = 10 A
  • Power factor = 0.90
  • Operating time = 8 hours

Real power = (1.732 × 415 × 10 × 0.90) ÷ 1,000

Real power ≈ 6.47 kW

Energy for 8 hours = 6.47 × 8 = approximately 51.8 kWh.

This is an estimate based on constant load, voltage, current and power factor. Actual consumption changes when the load varies during operation.

3. How to calculate consumption from an energy meter

If a properly installed three-phase energy meter displays cumulative kWh, the simplest consumption calculation is:

Energy consumed = Current meter reading − Previous meter reading

Example: 12,845.6 kWh − 12,610.2 kWh = 235.4 kWh.

For a CT-operated meter, confirm the meter configuration and CT ratio. Some meters display primary-side energy after the CT ratio has been configured; others may require an external multiplier. Follow the meter manufacturer's instructions and the installation's metering scheme to avoid applying the CT multiplier twice.

4. What if the three-phase load is unbalanced?

The simplified √3 formula assumes a balanced three-phase system. If phase currents or phase power factors differ significantly, do not treat one phase's current as representative of all three phases. Use a suitable three-phase power analyser or a meter that calculates total real power from the individual phase voltage and current measurements.

For a four-wire system with phase-to-neutral measurements, total real power can be determined by adding the real power of each phase, provided the instrument measures and reports the phase values correctly.

5. Common calculation mistakes

  • Using phase-to-neutral voltage in the balanced line-voltage formula.
  • Ignoring power factor and treating kVA as kW.
  • Multiplying by three when using a formula that already includes √3.
  • Assuming a motor or other variable load draws its rated current continuously.
  • Applying a CT ratio multiplier twice, or not applying it when the meter requires it.
  • Confusing kW (power) with kWh (energy).

6. kW, kVA and kWh: the difference

  • kW: Real power, the rate at which useful electrical work is performed.
  • kVA: Apparent power, based on voltage and current without the power-factor reduction.
  • kWh: Electrical energy accumulated over time; this is the unit commonly used for billing.

For a sinusoidal balanced load, kW = kVA × power factor. With nonlinear loads and significant harmonics, use true power and true power factor readings from an appropriate meter.

Frequently asked questions

Can I calculate a monthly electricity bill from kW?

First estimate energy in kWh by multiplying average real power by operating hours. For an accurate bill estimate, account for changing loads, tariff slabs, fixed charges, taxes and other applicable billing components.

Does a higher power factor reduce kWh?

For the same useful real energy delivered to a load, improving power factor does not automatically reduce the load's kWh requirement. It can reduce current and associated system losses, and may help avoid utility power-factor penalties where applicable.

Can I use this formula for a single-phase load?

No. For a single-phase load, the usual real-power estimate is kW = (Voltage × Current × Power factor) ÷ 1,000. Use the appropriate measured values and consider waveform distortion where relevant.
